# PawBooker — Worker Classification: Factual Brief

## What the business is
PawBooker is a **technology platform (mobile app)** that connects pet owners
("Customers") with **independent pet-grooming businesses** ("Groomers"). Groomers
are typically pre-existing salons or self-employed groomers with their own
brand, location, and clientele. PawBooker provides software for discovery,
scheduling, messaging, and payment processing, and earns revenue from platform
fees and an optional subscription. **PawBooker does not itself groom pets.**

## How the relationship actually works (all verifiable in the product)

**Behavioral control — Groomer controls the *means and manner* of work:**
- Groomers set **their own service menu and prices**; PawBooker does not set prices.
- Groomers set **their own business hours and availability**.
- Groomers **decide which booking requests to accept, decline, or cancel** — no
  jobs are assigned or dispatched to them by PawBooker.
- Groomers use **their own tools, supplies, and equipment**.
- Groomers perform work at **their own location** (their salon), not a PawBooker premises.
- PawBooker provides **no training** on how to groom and does not supervise the work.

**Financial control — Groomer bears the economics of an independent business:**
- Each Groomer connects **their own bank account** via Stripe Connect and receives
  payouts directly; PawBooker does not pay wages or an hourly/salary rate.
- Groomers **pay PawBooker fees** (a per-booking platform fee, a one-time
  acquisition fee on the first booking of an app-sourced customer, and an optional
  "Pro" subscription) — the money flows from Groomer to platform, not the reverse.
- Groomers are **responsible for their own taxes**; they receive Stripe/1099 tax
  forms rather than a W-2, and PawBooker does not withhold payroll taxes.
- Groomers are **responsible for their own business licensing and insurance**;
  PawBooker does not provide either and does not verify them.
- Groomers have their own opportunity for profit/loss (they set prices, manage
  their own costs, and keep their own margins).

**Relationship type — indicators of independence:**
- Groomers operate under **their own business name, bio, and branding** in the app.
- Groomers **employ their own staff** (the app has a salon-staff feature; the
  Groomer, not PawBooker, is those workers' employer).
- Groomers **bring and keep their own customers** (an invite-code feature lets a
  Groomer onboard their existing clientele; those bookings are fee-exempt).
- Nothing requires exclusivity — Groomers are free to serve walk-ins, use other
  booking tools, and maintain business outside PawBooker.
- The customer's service contract is **directly between Customer and Groomer**;
  PawBooker's Terms state PawBooker is not a party to the grooming service and is
  not the Groomer's employer, agent, partner, or franchisee.

## Areas we want counsel to pressure-test (stated honestly)
These are the facts a regulator might point to; we want to understand their weight:
1. **PawBooker sits in the payment flow and takes a cut** (platform fee + acquisition
   fee). This is a *marketplace-facilitator* characteristic — does it bear on
   employment classification, or only on marketplace/sales-tax obligations?
2. **PawBooker calculates and collects applicable sales tax** on transactions.
3. **PawBooker operates platform governance** — it can suspend/remove Groomers for
   fraud or serious complaints, hosts reviews, and handles reported issues. Does
   platform-integrity enforcement risk being read as "control"?
4. **ABC test, Prong B** (work outside the hiring entity's usual course of business):
   PawBooker's business is a software/marketplace platform, and grooming is a
   different trade — we believe this cuts in our favor, but want it confirmed,
   including how our marketing/brand language should describe the service.

## Questions for counsel
- Under our state's test (and California's ABC test if we operate/expand there),
  are Groomers properly classified as independent businesses / contractors?
- Does anything in how we operate today create employment-classification exposure,
  and what specific changes (product, contract, or marketing) would reduce it?
- Is "marketplace vs. SaaS" purely a sales-tax/consumer-law question, or does it
  interact with the employment question at all?
- What contract language or operational guardrails best preserve independent-
  contractor status as we grow?
